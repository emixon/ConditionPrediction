\section{Introduction}

Sustainment of very large real property portfolios requires many complex decisions. When a few decision makers must allocate constrained maintenance funding across millions of facilities, informed individual repair/replace decisions become infeasible for the whole portfolio. Real property managers in this situation rely on mathematical models to assist with funding distribution and work action decisions across the large scope.  These sustainment decision models generally rely on condition information about the inventory within the portfolio to drive their decision-making. This is known as a knowledge-based decision model.  In many cases, it is infeasible to install condition sensors on every component of every facility, so knowledge-based models will use expert observations in the form of condition assessments to inform inventory condition.  While sustainment models can attempt to predict condition deterioration over time, much like weather prediction models, they are only reliable for short-term horizons. So, periodic re-observations are required through assessment contracts with large architecture and engineering (A\&E) firms to collect and update condition information.  

Stemming from these requirements comes a need for real property managers to decide what inventory in their portfolio is due for re-observation.  Historically, the most common method is to simply reassess everything every 5 years (i.e. 20\% of the portfolio per year).  To reduce the amount and therefore cost of these assessments, a value of information (VOI) strategy could be utilized to make informed selections of which assets or components within the inventory are of the highest value to re-assess (i.e. the most likely to improve our maintenance investment decisions).  A critical requirement of this model is to be able to predict what scores an assessor would likely assign to a component based on historical data. 

\subsection{Dataset}

The dataset used for this project comes from assessment data in the Sustainment Management System (SMS) BUILDER platform developed by Construction Engineering Research Laboratory (CERL). The full dataset includes approximately 2.7 million observations. Observations include both a manually collected direct condition rating and a predicted condition index generated from component information using the SMS Weibull degradation model at the time of an assessment. Multiple observations can be generated for given components if they have multiple assessment records. The observation's predicted Condition Index (CI) is generated using the following:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sms-weibull-ci}
\mathrm{CI}(t) = A \left( \frac{100}{\mathrm{CI}_{\mathrm{T}}} \right)^{ -\left(\frac{t}{\beta}\right)^{\alpha} }
\end{equation}

Where $\mathrm{t}$ is the normalized age of the component, $\mathrm{CI_T}$ is the design life of the component, A is the initial starting condition (usually 100), $\alpha$ is the scale or characteristic age of replacement, and $\beta$ is the shape or degradation parameter. The $\alpha$ and $\beta$ parameters are provided from a per specification reference catalog within the SMS, however these values can be shifted after assessments to adjust the curve to fit to the assessed condition. In cases where components have multiple assessments, observations use the SMS $\alpha$ and $\beta$ shift calculations prior to calculating the predicted CI; this ensures continuity with the prediction at the time of the assessment. A detailed intuition for the Weibull model is available using the Desmos tool that can be found at \url{https://www.desmos.com/calculator/cenbpl3qtf}. The final resulting observations also capture other information about the component. See Table~\ref{table:assembly} for complete details.

Assessment scores generally are collected as "Direct Condition Ratings", amounting to a selection from 9 possible score options. An example criterion for these scores can be found below in Table \ref{table:AssessmentScores} (Not all organizations have exactly the same criterion, but the score choices are always the same). Most organizations have additional guidance through large (sometimes 1000's of pages) assessment guides. These guides direct the types of components to inventory and assess, when and how to input comments about the assessment, additional criterion for scoring for specific component types, and other data quality governance. 

\subsection{Proposed Objectives}

Although the dataset consists of 2.7 million observations, a subset corresponding to a single material equipment category (MEC) will be used for this project. The MEC for boilers consists of 12,832 assessments, so it should be possible to perform a detailed analysis for the specifications within this MEC. The goal of this study will be to identify the best parameters about a component that would inform the best possible predictions about a likely assessment score distribution.  The study could also explore different types of models such as binning solutions, decision trees (such as eXtreme Gradient Boosting, abbreviated XGBoost), artificial neural networks (ANNs), or other class-based solutions to find which of the options would inform the best predictions.

The proposed workflow for the project is:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Perform initial exploratory analysis to identify data wrangling needs and potential data bias.
\item Perform Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to identify the best predictive parameters.
\item Build a prediction model returning multi-class distribution using an ANN trained on identified principal components.
\item Build a prediction model returning multi-class distribution using XGBoost (or other decision tree library) trained on identified principal components.
\item Build additional prediction models based on in-class statistical learning solutions.
\item Perform comparative study between prediction models to identify which has the best predictive performance.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Exploratory Data Analysis}

\subsection{Basic Data Description and Initial Questions}

As a first step of exploratory analysis, the shapes of individual columns of the dataset were visualized to understand the distributions of the data. Although distributions were produced and explored for all fields in the dataset, only the distributions most relevant to the motivating questions outlined in this section are highlighted in this report. 

For the purposes of this project, two of the most pertinent columns in the dataset include the predicted condition score, or CI, of the Weibull degradation model and the actual observed score of the assessor (i.e., assessor rating).  Based on the histograms for these two variables, there is a visually identifiable difference between the expected degradation and the assessed scores (Figures \ref{fig:ci_dist} and \ref{fig:score_dist}). There also seems to be a heavy bias toward ratings of 88 in the assessed boiler condition scores compared to the Weibull-predicted CIs.

There are some rational narratives that can explain the disagreement in predicted and assessed scores.  The SMS system from which the observations are sourced is used by organizations to plan maintenance management.  Work analysis rules are put into place within the system to prioritize maintenance funding allocation.  For "D30" systems (mechanical systems in UNIFORMAT which includes boilers), a typical ruleset will specify that components with a CI less than 85 should be marked for repair action, and components with a remaining service life (RSL) of less than 2 should be marked for replacement. If maintenance dollars are being executed by that guidance, then it would be expected that assessors rarely come across boilers with condition less than 85.  This also makes rational sense from a facility function perspective, heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) systems tend to get immediate attention when they fail, as they are considered critical for most facilities. A score of 88 represents a well maintained and functioning unit, which is what is expected based on maintenance priority. Additionally, this bias is not unique to SMS data, and has been observed in other inspection annd assessment-based condition datasets. One such example is an Ontario bridge inspection dataset where a similar condition clustering is observed at around 77 (Based on their unique condition rating system) \cite{ontario_bridge_conditions_2021}.

Additionally, based on this visual inspection of the assessed score distribution, questions were formulated to attempt to identify how data could be classified to distinguish between boilers that are kept to specified condition versus those that are allowed to deteriorate. Answers to these questions may assist in feature selection for the future construction of a prediction model. Looking at several of the other distributions, including Remaining Service Life (RSL) (Figure \ref{fig:rsl_dist}), Month (Figure \ref{fig:month_dist}), and Climate (Figure \ref{fig:climate_dist}), raised possible considerations including:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Does RSL (or other lifecycle variables) correlate with assessor ratings?
    \item Does the month (i.e Season) of the assessment impact the expected assessed condition for boilers?
    \item Does the climate (warm or cold) impact expected assessed condition? (Note: Figure \ref{fig:doe_climate_map} illustrates the climate zones used in the dataset observations)
    \item Do specific facility types show maintenance priority?
    \item What other ways could the data be classified to help with prediction models?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Pairwise Correlation Analysis}
Next, a pairwise correlation analysis was completed to understand relationships among the features in the raw dataset. To this end, Spearman's rank correlation coefficient ($\rho$), which is a nonparametric measure of the strength and direction of the monotonic relationship between two variables, was calculated for each combination of pairs among the following set of fifteen features from the raw dataset: NormalizedAge, EffectiveAge, RSL, Recency, PrevScore, PrevAssessed, PredictedCI, Score, Qty, AssetAge, AssetDesignLife, LogCRV, Month, Year, and Climate. Figure \ref{fig:spearman_heatmap_with_sig}  visualizes the results of this analysis as a heatmap of correlation coefficients. Positive correlation coefficients, shown as red squares in the heatmap, indicate a positive monotonic relationship, while negative correlation coefficients, shown as blue squares in the heatmap, represent a negative monotonic relationship. Asterisks are used to denote the statistical significance of the correlation between variable pairs, with * signifying $p < 0.05$, ** signifying $p < 0.01$, and *** signifying $p < 0.001$.

Figure \ref{fig:spearman_heatmap_with_sig} shows the strongest pairwise correlations between PredictedCI and NormalizedAge, EffectiveAge, and RSL, as well as between RSL and NormalizedAge. The calculated coefficients for these relationships were -0.88, -0.81, 0.91, and -0.90, respectively, all with statistical significance at p < 0.001. However, these relationships are expected based on the characteristics of the dataset. For example, NormalizedAge is an input to the equation used to calculate PredictedCI, and EffectiveAge is used to calculate RSL. These correlations among dependent variables will need to be mitigated during the development of predictive models in order to prevent confounding.

In contrast, the relative strength of pairwise correlations with the Score variable, which represents an Expert assessor's assigned condition rating of a given boiler, are lower. For example, the correlation coefficients with the largest magnitude are those with RSL, NormalizedAge, and PredictedCI, but their values are only 0.54, -0.53, and 0.50, respectively, each with statistical significance at p < 0.001. These results indicate that Score cannot be predicted by a single correlation with another variable, and that a multivariate approach is needed to understand the complex relationships influencing Score.

As another way of visualizing these correlations among variables, a correlation plot was generated for the same fifteen features from the raw dataset. A thumbnail of this correlation plot is included as Figure \ref{fig:boiler_corr_plot} in this report. However, since the plot is large, the full-sized version can be found in our GitHub repository under figures/boiler\_corr\_plot.png

\subsection{Data Wrangling}

After initial visualization and correlation analysis steps, the data were cleaned to prepare for further analysis. Table \ref{tab:boiler_descriptive_statistics} shows some basic statistics of the raw dataset. Notice that some columns such as Recency, Climate, and PrevScore have missing values. Additionally, some columns represent non-ordinal identifiers that are not typically useful in analyses such as PCA, and most of the ordinal columns are in mixed types and ranges varying by several orders of magnitude. To address these issues, a series of data wrangling steps were performed:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item For Recency and PrevScore, the missing data represents components that have never previously been assessed. Since "Never Assessed" is potentially a valuable data point, the first step is to create a new column called "PrevAssessed" with a value of 0 or 1 representing whether a component has been assessed before.  Missing values are then replaced with the mean of available data in both Recency and PrevScore, respectively.
    \item For Climate, the missing data is simply missing data, with no underlying significance to its absence. Therefore, the missing fields are addressed by populating them with the mean of the known climate data.
    \item AssetAge and AssetDesignLife are better represented as a single property similar to the component NormalizedAge.  A new column is created called NormalizedAssetAge set to AssetAge / AssetDesignLife.
    \item For Month, since we are interested in impact of seasons on assessment scores, it is better to represent this data in a way that keeps the cyclical nature of seasons intact. Two new columns are created (MonthSin and MonthCos) representing the Month in a circular projection and keeping months, like December and January, close together ordinally.
    \item Component Replacement Value (CRV) is several orders of magnitude larger than other data fields. This may be problematic because even with data standardization prior to PCA, CRV may still be ordinally dominating. To address this, a new column is created called LogCRV representing the log of the original CRV values.  This brings the scale to a more comparable space and also makes a more symmetric dataset as can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:crv_log}
    \item Id, Spec, System, MEC, UOM, Fac, Qty, DesignLife, Month, AssetAge, and AssetDesignLife are omitted from the PCA analysis due to either being non-ordinal identifier columns, having the same value across nearly all the dataset (Qty, DesignLife), or having alternative suitable representations from the above wrangling steps.  Score is also omitted in the interest of avoiding data leakage when back loading for analysis of potential predictive features. 
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Principal Component Analysis}

After data wrangling, a PCA was performed using the following columns of the cleaned dataset: NormlizedAge, EffectiveAge, RSL, PredictedCI, Recency, PrevScore, PrevAssessed, NormalizedAssetAge, LogCRV, Year, MonthSin, MonthCos, and Climate.  First, data were normalized and standardized (by centering on the mean, then dividing by standard deviation). Then, the singular value decomposiion SVD was generated. Figure \ref{fig:pca_init} shows the initial explained variance by principal component. Principal component (PC) 1 has a notable dominance of 34\% of the explained variance. Upon inspection of PC 1, all similarly derived lifecycle parameters (NormalizedAge, RSL, PredictedCI, and EffectiveAge) have roughly equal absolute values of ~.49 and are therefore relatively redundant. A new version of the PCA was then run using a single lifecycle parameter, RSL. RSL was selected because it is an intuitively interpretable value. The resulting PCA produced a smoother decline in explained variance across the PCs, which can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:pca_reduced}. Of note, a third PCA version was run that included all lifecycle parameters divided by 4 to reduce overall influence, but the results were unremarkable (refer to Figure \ref{fig:scaled_lifecycle_pca}). Therefore, further discussion of the PCA will refer to the reduced lifecycle version that was performed using RSL as the representative parameter.

The loadings heatmap of the reduced-parameter PCA (Figure \ref{fig:heatmap_reduced_pca}) still shows the expected dominance of RSL in the first PC, but Recency, Climate, and Month are also key to PCs 2 and 3. To search for potential predictability, PC observation models (Vt') values were mapped back to assessed scores, color coded by "Green Region" (score >= 85) and "Not Green Region" (score < 85) and then plotted in histograms for each PC. Figure \ref{fig:score_dist_by_pc_reduced_pca} shows the results. Unfortunately, the analysis resulted in no clear distinguishing characteristics. PC 1, dominated by RSL, shows the most promise, but still has heavy overlap. Similar analysis was done for all 81 pairings of PC Scores, color coding by region and plotting as scatter plots. No definitive separation of clusters was apparent. The resulting mega plot is omitted from this document, but be found in the repository under figures/pc\_vs\_pc\_score\_scatter.png.

Additional analysis was performed that attempted to include Facility (Fac) and Specification (Spec) information through hot matrix. With ~134 Fac codes and ~110 Specifications though, variance was spread across too many columns. Additional PC 1 histograms were produced with various trial and error parameter sets, attempting to further identify combinations that better separated Green and Not Green regions. Through the trials, it seemed that reintroducing PredictedCI (in addition to RSL) helped remove the two-peaked distribution in the Green data and represented more offset picture of two normal distributions. Other combinations of lifecycle parameters were tried with less desirable results. Additionally, better separation was observed when omitting PrevScore and Recency. It appears that PrevAssessed alone produced observably better separation. PrevScore, Recency, and PrevAssessed are linked, similar to the lifecycle parameters, although it is unclear as to why the simple boolean of PrevAssesed produced better separation between clusters.

A new column was also introduced called "PercentGreen", as a way of representing Facility (Fac) code in the PCA.  It was produced by grouping the observations by Fac and then calculating the percentage of observations greater than or equal to 88 for each unique Fac. The result was then assigned as a new column across all observations. The rationale was that the Fac code may help to uderstand the relative importance of a particular facility type (Figure \ref{fig:not_green_fac} shows the variability in Fac codes with more than 20 observations). However, limitations to this approach include the fact that some Fac codes only have 1-2 assessments, which may not be a sufficient representation for a meaningful score, and additionally, the new column could be introducing a source of data leakage in the model since it derived using the dependent variable. Therefore, we used the new column with caution, and will verify its legitimacy in future project steps when we perform cross-validation of our prediction models. Of note, however, the new column did provide minor additional separation of regions in our histogram.

The most promising feature combinations are shown in Figures \ref{fig:rsl_no_pgreen}, \ref{fig:rsl_w_pgreen}, \ref{fig:rsl_ci_no_pgreen}, and \ref{fig:rsl_ci_w_pgreen}, where we test various feature combinations including RSL, PredictedCI, and PercentGreen.

Based on this analysis, future feature selection for predictive models can begin with the following guidelines:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Lifecycle parameters limited to RSL and PredictedCI or both.
    \item Assessment history data limited to simply "PrevAssessed".
    \item LogCRV, MonthSin, MonthCos, NormalizedAssetAge, and Climate all provide reasonable and roughly equivalent contributions.
    \item PercentGreen or other Fac/Spec representation may be useful if shown not to cause data leakage or overfitting through cross validation.
    \item Qty and Year provide negligible influence and can be omitted in favor of model simplification.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Additional Data Insights}
% Julian
The following analyses compare green ($\mathrm{Score}\geq 88$) and not-green
($\mathrm{Score}<88$) assessment records in terms of lifecycle characteristics,
asset age, climate, season of assessment, and prediction error. Observations
may include repeated assessments of the same component.

\subsubsection{Lifecycle Characteristics and Assessment History}

Remaining service life (RSL) and previous assessment scores provide two perspectives on the relationship between asset history and current condition (Figure~\ref{fig:julian_plot1}). Green assessments have a substantially higher average RSL (18.61) than not-green assessments (8.76), while their average previous scores are 88.56 and 79.39, respectively. The differences between these distributions indicate that both lifecycle characteristics and previous condition may contain useful information for predicting assessment scores. Although the distributions overlap, the general separation between the groups supports further consideration of these variables in predictive modeling.

\subsubsection{Condition Status Across Asset-Age Groups}

Asset age is another important characteristic when examining differences in component condition (Figure~\ref{fig:julian_plot2}). The proportion of not-green assessments generally increases with age, rising from approximately 10.9\% for assets younger than 10 to 30.3\% for those between 30 and 40, before decreasing to 24.1\% for assets aged 40 or older. Although the relationship is not strictly increasing, the results indicate that asset age may help explain variations in assessed condition. The lower proportion among the oldest assets also suggests that age alone is insufficient to describe condition, highlighting the potential importance of other asset characteristics and assessment history.

\subsubsection{Condition Status Across Climate Categories}

Differences in assessed condition are also apparent across climate categories (Figure~\ref{fig:julian_plot3}). Climate Codes 4 and 8 have relatively high proportions of not-green assessments, approximately 50.0\% and 46.1\%, respectively, compared with only 12.4\% for Climate Code 7. This variation indicates that climate classification may provide additional information when evaluating component condition. However, nearly half of the observations have missing climate information, and the differences between categories may also reflect variations in asset age, maintenance history, or other characteristics. Further analysis is therefore necessary to determine whether climate contributes independently to condition prediction.

\subsubsection{Seasonal Variation in Condition Status}

The climate comparison is extended by grouping assessments into winter, spring, summer, and fall to examine potential seasonal patterns in recorded condition (Figure~\ref{fig:julian_plot4}). For Climate Code 5, the proportion of not-green assessments increases from approximately 10.8\% during summer to 24.0\% during fall, indicating differences in condition distributions between assessment periods. Similar comparisons across the remaining climate categories provide a broader perspective on how condition status varies throughout the year. Groups containing fewer than 50 observations are identified separately to avoid overinterpreting limited data. These seasonal differences may also reflect assessment timing and asset characteristics rather than direct environmental effects.

\subsubsection{Prediction Error Across Asset-Age Groups}

Prediction performance is examined across asset-age groups to determine whether the agreement between predicted condition indices (PredictedCI) and recorded assessment scores varies with age (Figure~\ref{fig:julian_plot6}). Mean absolute error increases from approximately 6.76 points for assets younger than 10 to 18.21 points for assets between 20 and 30, indicating that prediction accuracy is not uniform across age groups. Mean bias provides additional insight into whether condition is systematically overestimated or underestimated. These findings highlight potential limitations of the existing condition estimates and suggest that accounting for differences in asset characteristics may be important for improving future predictive models.

\subsection{Next Steps}
This exploratory analysis builds the foundations for future construction of predictive models by elucidating key characteristics and limitations of the Boilers dataset. For example, a bias around 88 in the assessed boiler condition scores when compared to the Wiebull-predicted CIs was identified as a direct consequence of organizational maintenance prioritization. This will potentially need to be accounted for in future dataset modeling. Additionally, correlation among independent variables, particularly the boiler lifecycle parameters, was quantified using Spearman's rank correlation coefficients. Similarly, these correlations will need to be addressed to mitigate confounding in future predictive models. The calculated Spearman correlation coefficients also demonstrated that a boiler's condition Score cannot be predicted by a single correlation with another variable, justifying the multivariate approaches outlined in the Proposed Objectives section. Data wrangling prepared the data for PCA, and promising feature combinations were identified using PCA. PCA results were also used to develop guidelines for feature selection for future predictive models. Finally, additional analyses compared green ($\mathrm{Score}\geq 88$) and not-green
($\mathrm{Score}<88$) assessment records in terms of lifecycle characteristics, asset age, climate, season of assessment, and prediction error in order to understand how this grouping might affect trends in the dataset. 

The findings of this exploratory analysis will be directly used in the next steps of our proposed workflow, including development of prediction models using ANNs and decision-tree libraries trained on identified PCs.
